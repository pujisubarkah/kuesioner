## Abstract

This study investigates regional disparities, infrastructural friction, and occupational workload constraints in digital competency development among Indonesian civil servants. Employing an explanatory cross-sectional survey design with inferential modeling and qualitative narrative triangulation, this study analyzed a nationwide dataset of 4,091 civil servants across 38 provinces. Data were collected via a structured online instrument measuring technical disruptions, device affordances, operational workload collision, and synchronous training behaviors. Inferential analysis was conducted using One-Way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis tests, and Multiple Linear Regression modeling. The findings demonstrate that digital learning friction is driven not solely by infrastructural connectivity failures (66.5% experiencing connection drops), but predominantly by occupational workload overlap ($M = 3.88/5.00, SD = 0.88$), which induces widespread multitasking during synchronous sessions ($M = 3.58, SD = 0.91$). Macro-regional analysis revealed significant spatial disparities in technical disruption ($F(5, 4047) = 9.41, p < .001, \eta^2 = 0.012$), with Java recording significantly higher stability than off-Java islands (e.g., Riau Islands, Central Kalimantan, Aceh, and Maluku). Typological comparisons across 3,898 classified respondents showed significant but modest differences in technical disruption ($F(4, 3887) = 7.48, p < .001, \eta^2 = 0.008$) and workload collision ($F(4, 3897) = 5.46, p < .001, \eta^2 = 0.006$), with remote ($M = 3.30$) and archipelagic ($M = 3.14$) areas experiencing the highest disruption. Multiple regression analysis ($R^2 = 0.429, F(6, 4048) = 506.67, p < .001$) revealed that supervisor support for dedicated study time ($\beta = 0.407, p < .001$) and device ease of access ($\beta = 0.281, p < .001$) are the primary determinants of learning focus, while technical disruption frequency becomes non-significant when organizational conversion factors are controlled. The study recommends transitioning national public sector training from bandwidth-heavy synchronous webinars toward asynchronous micro-learning, eliminating punitive camera-on requirements, and legally institutionalizing duty-free learning hours.

**Keywords:** *digital divide; public sector e-learning; civil servants; workload collision; spatial disparities; macro-regional inequality; synchronous training.*

## 1. Introduction

Digital learning has emerged as the central paradigm for professional development and competency enhancement in modern public sector governance. Its primary appeal lies in cost-effective scalability: training programs can be rapidly disseminated across dispersed administrative jurisdictions without the prohibitive logistical expenditures of conventional classroom-based residential training. However, the rapid expansion of e-learning platforms does not automatically yield equitable educational participation. Contemporary scholarship indicates that digital education often reproduces or even amplifies structural inequalities when learners differ systematically in the stability of their telecommunications infrastructure, the affordances of their physical hardware, their digital capabilities, and the institutional conditions of their workplace environments (Miras et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2025; UNESCO, 2023a; van de Werfhorst et al., 2022).

In the digital divide literature, theoretical conceptualizations have shifted beyond a simplistic binary distinction between users and non-users. Contemporary frameworks conceptualize digital inequality across three cumulative levels: the *first-level divide* concerning material access (hardware, connection quality, bandwidth, and electricity stability); the *second-level divide* concerning digital literacy, competencies, and usage patterns; and the *third-level divide* concerning the tangible outcomes, professional benefits, and learning conversion that individuals extract from digital participation (Helsper, 2021; Lythreatis et al., 2022; van Dijk, 2020). For public sector employees, material access is not merely a question of whether a learning portal can be resolved in a web browser, but whether the digital experience can support continuous cognitive engagement while official public service duties are concurrently performed.

This multidimensional perspective is exceptionally acute in Indonesia. As the world's largest archipelagic nation, Indonesia exhibits substantial geographic and infrastructural heterogeneity across its 38 provinces (Choczyńska, 2024; Jaya et al., 2024; Saksono, 2024; Wati et al., 2024). While central metropolitan nodes enjoy robust high-speed optical broadband, peripheral islands, frontier zones, and rural districts frequently contend with fragile cellular backhauls, periodic power outages, and shared office bandwidth. However, geography is not the sole determinant of educational marginalization. Organizational arrangements, supervisor attitudes, workload intensity, and the pedagogical design of public sector training programs act as critical "conversion factors" (Sen, 1999) that determine whether nominal access is converted into meaningful competency acquisition.

Although existing empirical studies on Indonesian public sector modernization acknowledge infrastructure and digital literacy constraints (Silitonga, 2023; Sutrisno et al., 2024; Yaqin et al., 2023), few investigations systematically evaluate the empirical collision between digital learning modalities and authentic workplace routines across both macro-geographic regions and micro-workplace typologies. Civil servants are frequently mandated to participate in multi-hour synchronous webinars while remaining fully responsible for front-line public administration and citizen services. 

To address these empirical and methodological gaps, this study investigates the intersection of infrastructural quality, device affordances, occupational workload collision, and regional disparities using a nationwide dataset of 4,091 Indonesian civil servants. Specifically, this study addresses three core research questions:
1. **RQ1:** What is the empirical profile of infrastructure, device, operational workload, and synchronous training constraints experienced by Indonesian civil servants?
2. **RQ2:** Do technical disruptions, workload overlap, and learning constraints differ significantly across self-reported regional typologies (Urban, Rural, Archipelagic, Border, and Remote) and macro-geographic island clusters?
3. **RQ3:** To what extent do infrastructural disruptions, device accessibility, and organizational conversion factors (supervisor support, workload overlap) predict civil servants' cognitive focus during digital learning?

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Digital Divide as a Multilevel Phenomenon
This study conceptualizes digital learning disparities through van Dijk's (2020) and Helsper's (2021) multilevel digital divide theory. The first level encompasses material access, operationalized through the International Telecommunication Union's (ITU, 2023a) framework of *Meaningful Connectivity*—which demands not merely nominal internet presence, but adequate bandwidth, device sufficiency, data affordability, and network reliability. The second and third levels address skills, usage forms, and offline outcome conversion. In adult professional e-learning, access does not guarantee participation, and participation does not guarantee cognitive absorption.

### 2.2 Workplace Conversion Factors and Cognitive Load Theory
Within the capability approach and adult learning theory (Knowles, 1984; Sen, 1999), organizational conditions serve as *conversion factors* that enable or restrict the transformation of technological resources into human capabilities. In civil service contexts, protected learning time, supervisor backing, and workload flexibility are necessary conversion factors. 

When institutional support is absent, learners experience severe cognitive conflict. According to Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller et al., 2019), human working memory has strictly limited processing capacity. When public servants must attend synchronous lecture streams while simultaneously responding to administrative tasks, incoming citizen queries, or drafting official documents, the resulting *split-attention effect* imposes massive extraneous cognitive load. Coping mechanisms—such as switching to smartphones, turning off webcams, and backgrounding video feeds—represent rational behavioral adaptations to cognitive and bandwidth constraints rather than personal negligence.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Research Design and Sampling
This study adopted an explanatory cross-sectional survey design combining quantitative inferential modeling with qualitative open-narrative triangulation. The sampling frame targeted Indonesian civil servants (PNS and PPPK) across central ministries, provincial administrations, and municipal/regency governments across all 38 provinces. 

Data collection was executed via a centralized web-based instrument backed by a high-availability PostgreSQL database. A total of **$N = 4,091$** validated responses were captured. To ensure statistical integrity in comparative typology tests, a total of 193 responses (167 respondents who categorized their area as "Lainnya/Other" and 18 unspecified responses) were handled systematically: they were retained for overall descriptive, regression, and macro-regional island analyses ($N = 4,091$), but excluded from specific five-class discrete typology comparative tests, yielding a clean analytical sample of **$n = 3,898$** across defined regional typologies.

### 3.2 Instrumentation
The survey comprised 33 structured items structured into eight conceptual dimensions:
1. *Demographic & Spatial Profile (Q1–Q5):* Province, regency, self-identified duty typology (Urban, Rural, Archipelagic, Border, Remote/3T), institution tier, age group, and tenure.
2. *E-Learning History & Modalities (Q6–Q8):* Previous platform engagement, MOOCs, and synchronous webinars.
3. *Devices & Connectivity Quality (Q9–Q14):* Primary hardware, connectivity sources, disruption frequency (Q12, 5-point scale), disruption typology (Q13, multiple choice), and qualitative disruption logs (Q14).
4. *Workplace Environment & Operational Workload (Q15–Q19):* Workload overlap during training (Q15), service-mandated dropouts (Q16), time sufficiency (Q17), supervisor support for learning allocation (Q18), and physical workplace mobility (Q19) (5-point Likert scales, 1 = Strongly Disagree/Never, 5 = Strongly Agree/Always).
5. *Material Affordances & Usability (Q20–Q24):* Device access ease (Q20), file size/buffering barriers (Q21), format suitability (Q24).
6. *Interaction Dynamics & Focus (Q25–Q28):* Q&A opportunities (Q25), peer collaboration (Q26), and subjective learning focus level (Q27).
7. *Synchronous Session Behaviors (Q33 Matrix):* Frequency of camera-on, camera-off, smartphone usage, task multitasking, and disconnects (1–5 scale).
8. *Grounded Narrative Reflections (Q29–Q32):* Open-ended accounts of systemic bottlenecks, desired pedagogical changes, and regional infrastructural realities.

### 3.3 Data Analysis Procedures
Data were analyzed using the following analytical pipeline:
* **RQ1 (Descriptive Profile):** Means, standard deviations, and percentage distributions across all items, including technical failure modes and synchronous behaviors.
* **RQ2 (Spatial & Typological Comparative Tests):** Evaluated across the 5 regional typologies ($n = 3,898$) and 6 macro-geographic island clusters ($N = 4,067$). Because sample sizes were unequal across categories, both parametric **One-Way ANOVA** (with $F$-statistic, degrees of freedom, and eta-squared $\eta^2$) and non-parametric **Kruskal-Wallis tests** (with $H$-statistic and epsilon-squared $\epsilon^2$) were executed. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted to evaluate pairwise disparities.
* **RQ3 (Inferential Relational Modeling):** Evaluated through Pearson correlation matrices and **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Multiple Linear Regression** predicting Learning Focus (Q27). Model diagnostics, $R^2$, Adjusted $R^2$, ANOVA $F$-statistic, unstandardized coefficients ($B, SE$), standardized beta ($\beta$), $t$-statistics, $p$-values, and $95\%$ confidence intervals were computed.
* **Qualitative Narrative Triangulation:** Open-ended qualitative accounts (Q14, Q29, Q30, Q31, Q32) were thematically categorized to provide contextual triangulation for the statistical patterns.

Crucially, device ease (Q20, $p = .081$) and self-reported learning focus (Q27, $p = .383$) did not differ significantly across typologies, demonstrating that hardware availability and personal learning motivation are relatively uniform across the Indonesian civil service.

### 4.5 Qualitative Narrative Triangulation

Qualitative reflections from open-ended survey items (Q14, Q29, Q31, Q32) corroborated and contextualized the empirical statistical findings:

1. **Infrastructural and Power Instability in Island/Peripheral Zones:**
   > *"Our area frequently experiences power outages, and when the electricity goes out, the Wi-Fi connection is lost. There is no backup generator in the sub-district office, so training stops midway and we must tether via personal cellular data, which is slow and costly."* (Civil Servant #3, Nias Islands, North Sumatra).
   > *"The distinct challenge here is geography and limited cellular towers... During webinars, the audio cuts out repeatedly, making it impossible to follow discussions."* (Civil Servant #8, Sorong, Southwest Papua).

2. **The Double Burden of Chronic Regional Understaffing:**
   > *"In remote regencies, personnel shortages mean one officer handles multiple administrative functions. While attending online training, citizen service queues cannot be put on hold. Training providers must understand that civil servants cannot simply disconnect from front-line duties for 6 hours."* (Civil Servant #12, East Nusa Tenggara).

3. **Demands for Micro-Learning and Active Pedagogies:**
   > *"Replace 4-hour monologue webinars with practical case studies, simulations, and asynchronous 5-minute microlearning videos that can be accessed flexibly outside peak service hours."* (Civil Servant #1, West Lombok, NTB; Civil Servant #5, South Sulawesi).

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Re-Examining the "Spatial Inequality" Paradox
The empirical evidence resolves a key conceptual ambiguity in public sector digital transformation. While telecommunications infrastructure exhibits significant macro-spatial divergence across island clusters ($F = 9.41, p < .001$)—with Java maintaining superior network resilience over Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, and Papua—the actual friction experienced by individual learners is fundamentally organizational. The effect size of regional typologies on disruption is modest ($\eta^2 = 0.008$), demonstrating that infrastructural vulnerability is not confined to remote borderlands but extends into provincial capitals and peri-urban offices where local government bandwidth is heavily congested.

### 5.2 Cognitive Split-Attention and Rational Adaptations
Viewed through Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller et al., 2019), mandatory multi-hour synchronous webinars impose massive extraneous cognitive load when superimposed onto active public service duties ($M = 3.88$). In response, civil servants engage in rational self-regulation: turning off webcams ($M = 3.04$) and switching to mobile devices ($M = 3.16$) to manage concurrent document processing and public services. Training organizers often misinterpret these behaviors as indiscipline; however, as Castelli and Sarvary (2021) and Blikstein and Worsley (2016) note, behavioral proxies in digital spaces must be evaluated within their situated physical context.

### 5.3 Institutional Support as the Core Conversion Factor
The regression findings provide decisive empirical backing for Sen's (1999) Capability Approach. Technology access alone (Level 1 divide) does not translate into learning focus (Level 3 divide) unless mediated by institutional conversion factors. Supervisor backing for protected study time ($\beta = 0.407$) is nearly 1.5 times more impactful than physical device convenience ($\beta = 0.281$). Without organizational legitimacy to disconnect from routine duties, digital learning inevitably degenerates into superficial compliance and ghost learning.

## 6. Policy Implications and Strategic Recommendations

These empirical findings provide actionable recommendations for the National Institute of Public Administration (LAN RI), ministerial training bodies (BPSDM), and regional personnel agencies (BKD):

1. **Mandate Formal Protected Learning Time (Duty-Free Hours):** National civil service training regulations must legally require agency leaders to provide formal temporary relief from daily operational duties during accredited training modules.
2. **Shift from Monolithic Webinars to Asynchronous Microlearning:** Public training providers should systematically decompose 4–8 hour synchronous webinar marathons into modular, bite-sized units (3–5 minute videos, infographics, interactive scenario checks) that learners can complete asynchronously.
3. **Eliminate Discriminatory Synchronous Attendance Metrics:** Mandatory webcam rules should be formally abolished as a grading or attendance criterion, ensuring that civil servants in low-bandwidth, archipelagic, and resource-constrained regions are not systematically penalized.
4. **Deploy Bandwidth-Adaptive LMS Architectures:** Government platforms must implement progressive content loading, low-bitrate audio-first fallbacks, and offline-first caching for mobile users.

---

## 7. Limitations and Future Research

This study has two primary methodological limitations. First, data were collected via self-reported cross-sectional surveys, which may introduce subjective recall bias. Second, although the total dataset is substantial ($N = 4,091$), the strictly remote/3T subsample ($n = 30$) was relatively small due to severe connectivity barriers during online recruitment. Future research should implement automated, non-invasive telemetry analytics within government LMS platforms to empirically monitor clickstream behaviors and test adaptive, bandwidth-resilient micro-learning interventions in controlled field trials.

---

## 8. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that digital learning access among Indonesian civil servants is governed by an interdependent dynamic between macro-regional infrastructural disparities, hardware usability, and acute workplace workload constraints. While telecommunications reliability diverges across island clusters, the primary determinant of meaningful learning focus is institutional support and protected study time. Transforming public sector digital education requires moving beyond technocentric assumptions of frictionless e-learning and designing flexible, asynchronous, and context-aware learning architectures that respect the operational realities of public service delivery.
